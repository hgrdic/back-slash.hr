Human vs AI · Episode 1

AI writes correct Stripe signature checks now. Its example file still gives the secret away.

Builds run on 24 October 2026 with Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 in Claude Code 2.1.304.

I gave three Claude models the same request a founder would type: a Next.js app where users buy credits with Stripe Checkout and spend them. No mention of security. Then I audited the payment code the way I would for a client.

What they got right

All three verified the webhook signature on the raw request body, so a forged "payment succeeded" event with no signature is rejected. All three kept prices on the server, checked that the session was paid and the amount matched, recorded each checkout session so a replayed event never adds credits twice, and spent credits with a single atomic update. A year ago these were the mistakes I expected to find. Today's models handle them.

Where it still breaks: the secret, not the check

Each build shipped an .env.example with a placeholder webhook secret, and nothing in the code refuses to start with it. Copy that example to .env and forget to replace the secret, which is easy because the real one comes from a different place than your Stripe key, and the value "verifying" every webhook is a public string anyone can read in the repo. I signed three fake checkout.session.completed events with it and got 150 credits for nothing, on all three apps.

The telling part is that the app already knew how to do better. The success page after checkout calls stripe.checkout.sessions.retrieve to confirm the payment with Stripe directly. The webhook doesn't; it trusts whatever it just verified with the secret. So a weak secret is fatal on the webhook and invisible everywhere else. Two fixes a reviewer would add: refuse to boot on a placeholder or missing secret, and have the webhook fetch the session from Stripe again before crediting, the way the success page does.

What they all missed: money going back

When a purchase is refunded, Stripe sends charge.refunded; when a customer disputes the charge with their bank, it sends charge.dispute.created. None of the three listened for either. Pay $20 for 50 credits, get a full refund, keep every credit. This one needs no misconfiguration at all.

The AI fix

I described the refund problem to Opus 5.5 without hinting at a solution. Its fix stored the payment intent with each purchase, took back a matching share of credits on partial refunds, handled the same event arriving twice, and stopped the balance at zero rather than failing.

What still needs a human decision

A customer who spends all 50 credits and then disputes the charge ends at zero, with the account still open. Whether to lock the account, refund only unused credits or fight the dispute is a business rule, not a coding task. In the real world, a chargeback means the customer says they didn't intend the purchase or didn't get to use it, so it should be refunded, or at least the unused part. Unhappy paths like this only work when someone calls them out explicitly; otherwise they stay forgotten until a real user hits one.

Check your own app

  1. Is your webhook secret one you actually set, or still the example? Would the app even start if it were obviously a placeholder?
  2. Refund a test payment. Does anything change in your app?
  3. Open a test dispute (Stripe has a test card for it). What happens?
